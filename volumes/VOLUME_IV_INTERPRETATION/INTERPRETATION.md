## 1. Purpose

Volume IV governs how canonical objects may be understood, represented, compared, explained, translated, derived, contested, corrected, and reconstructed without loss of constitutional meaning.

Interpretation is necessary for participation.

Interpretation is constitutionally admissible only when its source, class, scope, authority, dependencies, provenance, uncertainty, and relationship to the canonical object remain explicit.

---

## 2. Interpretation Authority

The Morning Star Constitution governs this volume.

Volume II governs the identity and validity of interpretation objects.

Volume III governs the pathways through which interpretations are encountered and evaluated.

Volume IV governs interpretive admissibility.

It may not silently redefine the canonical source.

---

## 3. Interpretation Subject

The constitutional subject of Volume IV is:

> The governance of meaning derived from, applied to, compared with, translated from, or asserted about canonical Morning Star objects.

---

## 4. Interpretation Object

Every interpretation must possess:

- interpretation identifier;
- interpreted object identifier;
- interpretation class;
- interpreter identifier;
- source authority;
- source basis;
- declared scope;
- dependency set;
- provenance record;
- uncertainty class;
- equivalence claim;
- admissibility status;
- creation timestamp;
- revision timestamp;
- validation result.

An interpretation without an identifiable source object is inadmissible.

---

## 5. Interpretation Classes

The canonical interpretation classes are:

- CANONICAL
- LITERAL
- CONTEXTUAL
- DERIVED
- COMPARATIVE
- ANALOGICAL
- OPERATIONAL
- EDUCATIONAL
- TRANSLATIONAL
- SPECULATIVE
- CRITICAL
- CONFLICTING
- RECONSTRUCTED
- UNRESOLVED
- INADMISSIBLE

Interpretation class must remain explicit throughout the interpretation lifecycle.

---

## 6. Canonical Interpretation

An interpretation may be classified as CANONICAL only when:

- canonical authority is identified;
- the representation preserves the canonical definition;
- scope is preserved;
- dependency order is preserved;
- prohibited conflations are absent;
- provenance is complete;
- uncertainty is explicit;
- equivalence has been verified;
- the governing authority admits canonical status.

Authorship alone does not establish canonical status.

---

## 7. Literal Interpretation

A literal interpretation preserves the direct semantic expression of the source without material abstraction, extension, analogy, or contextual substitution.

Literal interpretation may still be incorrect when:

- the source is superseded;
- context is omitted;
- defined terms are altered;
- dependencies are ignored;
- scope is expanded;
- translation changes meaning.

---

## 8. Contextual Interpretation

A contextual interpretation explains a canonical object within a declared domain, circumstance, audience, or application setting.

Contextual interpretation must identify:

- the original canonical object;
- the contextual domain;
- retained meaning;
- altered presentation;
- excluded scope;
- domain-specific assumptions;
- unresolved differences.

Context must not silently replace canonical meaning.

---

## 9. Derived Interpretation

A derived interpretation produces a new claim, model, rule, implication, or application from one or more governed sources.

A derived interpretation must identify:

- derivation method;
- source objects;
- logical or procedural steps;
- assumptions;
- dependency requirements;
- evidence;
- uncertainty;
- limits of inference.

A derived interpretation must not inherit canonical authority automatically.

---

## 10. Comparative Interpretation

A comparative interpretation examines similarities and differences between governed objects.

Comparison must preserve:

- separate identities;
- separate authorities;
- separate scopes;
- separate dependencies;
- points of correspondence;
- points of divergence;
- unresolved equivalence.

Similarity does not establish identity.

Resonance does not establish derivation.

---

## 11. Analogical Interpretation

An analogy may support orientation or explanation.

An analogy must not be treated as proof, identity, equivalence, or authority.

Every analogy must identify:

- source domain;
- target domain;
- shared property;
- nonshared properties;
- explanatory purpose;
- breakdown conditions.

---

## 12. Operational Interpretation

An operational interpretation translates a canonical concept into a procedure, rule, test, interface behavior, data model, or executable condition.

Operational interpretation must preserve:

- governing concept;
- input conditions;
- transformation rule;
- output conditions;
- refusal behavior;
- uncertainty behavior;
- authority boundary;
- verification criteria.

Implementation convenience must not alter constitutional meaning.

---

## 13. Educational Interpretation

An educational interpretation adjusts presentation for comprehension.

Educational compression may simplify presentation.

It may not:

- remove constitutional dependencies;
- eliminate uncertainty;
- convert analogy into identity;
- imply authority that does not exist;
- erase scope;
- misstate framework relationships;
- represent partial understanding as competency.

---

## 14. Translational Interpretation

A translational interpretation converts meaning across:

- natural languages;
- technical vocabularies;
- disciplinary vocabularies;
- formal notation;
- code;
- visual representation;
- interface representation.

Translation must preserve:

- canonical identity;
- defined terms;
- scope;
- dependencies;
- uncertainty;
- authority;
- prohibited distinctions.

Translation loss must be recorded.

---

## 15. Speculative Interpretation

A speculative interpretation extends beyond verified evidence.

Speculative interpretations are admissible only when clearly labeled and when they preserve:

- source distinction;
- conjectural status;
- evidentiary limits;
- uncertainty;
- falsification conditions;
- noncanonical status.

Speculation must not be silently normalized.

---

## 16. Critical Interpretation

A critical interpretation evaluates, challenges, or disputes a governed object.

Critical interpretation is admissible when it identifies:

- object evaluated;
- claim challenged;
- governing standard;
- evidence;
- reasoning;
- scope;
- uncertainty;
- proposed alternative;
- authority status.

Disagreement is not itself semantic drift.

Misrepresentation of the object being criticized is semantic drift.

---

## 17. Conflicting Interpretation

Interpretations conflict when they make materially incompatible claims about the same governed object, scope, state, relationship, or authority.

Conflict must be preserved until resolved.

Conflict resolution may result in:

- one interpretation admitted;
- both interpretations admitted within different scopes;
- both rejected;
- conflict remaining unresolved;
- canonical source revision;
- semantic reconstruction.

---

## 18. Interpretation Equivalence

Canonical equivalence requires evidence that two representations preserve the same governed meaning within the same declared scope.

Equivalence assessment must evaluate:

- object identity;
- definition;
- scope;
- dependencies;
- authority;
- invariants;
- uncertainty;
- temporal version;
- prohibited conflations;
- expected consequences.

Lexical similarity alone is insufficient.

---

## 19. Interpretation Scope

Every interpretation must declare scope.

Scope dimensions may include:

- framework;
- object;
- domain;
- audience;
- jurisdiction;
- role;
- version;
- time;
- implementation;
- evidence set;
- application context.

Interpretation outside declared scope is not automatically admissible.

---

## 20. Interpretive Authority

Interpretive authority must identify:

- authority holder;
- authority source;
- authority type;
- governed scope;
- effective version;
- limitations;
- delegation;
- revocation conditions.

Competency may support interpretive reliability.

Competency does not independently create constitutional authority.

---

## 21. Interpretive Uncertainty

Interpretive uncertainty must distinguish:

- source uncertainty;
- translation uncertainty;
- contextual uncertainty;
- dependency uncertainty;
- evidentiary uncertainty;
- equivalence uncertainty;
- authority uncertainty;
- temporal uncertainty;
- implementation uncertainty.

A confidence score must not replace an uncertainty class.

---

## 22. Interpretation Drift

Interpretation drift exists when an interpretation loses or alters:

- source identity;
- canonical definition;
- scope;
- dependency order;
- authority;
- provenance;
- uncertainty;
- interpretation class;
- version;
- prohibited distinctions.

Drift may occur without intentional misconduct.

---

## 23. Interpretive Reconstruction

Interpretive reconstruction must:

1. identify the drifted interpretation;
2. preserve the original representation;
3. identify the violated invariant;
4. locate the authoritative source state;
5. identify corrective evidence;
6. reconstruct the interpretation;
7. preserve unresolved differences;
8. assign a resulting interpretation class;
9. trigger downstream revalidation;
10. record the reconstruction trace.

---

## 24. Interpretation Admission

Canonical admission decisions are:

- ADMISSIBLE
- ADMISSIBLE_WITH_LIMITS
- ADMISSIBLE_WITH_UNCERTAINTY
- PROVISIONAL
- CONFLICTING
- RECONSTRUCTION_REQUIRED
- UNRESOLVED
- INADMISSIBLE
- SUPERSEDED

Admission must be evidence-bearing and scope-bounded.

---

## 25. Interpretation Validation Order

Interpretation validation must occur in this order:

1. source object identity;
2. source object status;
3. interpretation identity;
4. interpretation class;
5. interpreter identity;
6. authority;
7. scope;
8. dependencies;
9. provenance;
10. uncertainty;
11. equivalence claim;
12. prohibited conflation review;
13. version currency;
14. constitutional invariant review;
15. admission decision.

A later stage may not override an earlier failure.

---

## 26. Role-Bounded Interpretation

Interpretation privileges may vary by participation role.

A role may determine whether a participant may:

- inspect;
- annotate;
- compare;
- derive;
- operationalize;
- publish;
- approve;
- revise;
- admit as canonical;
- reconstruct.

Role scope must not be inferred from technical access.

---

## 27. Interpretation Versioning

Every interpretation revision must preserve:

- prior version;
- new version;
- revision rationale;
- changed claims;
- changed scope;
- changed authority;
- changed evidence;
- changed uncertainty;
- dependency impact;
- admission impact.

Revision must not erase prior interpretive history.

---

## 28. Prohibited Interpretive Behavior

The following behavior is prohibited:

- presenting a derivative as canonical;
- hiding interpretation class;
- omitting source identity;
- expanding scope without evidence;
- suppressing uncertainty;
- claiming equivalence from similarity alone;
- converting analogy into proof;
- converting competency into authority;
- merging distinct frameworks;
- using superseded sources without disclosure;
- silently correcting drift;
- erasing conflicting interpretations;
- treating confidence as verification;
- treating repetition as canonicalization.

---

## 29. Deterministic Interpretation Evaluation

Given equivalent:

- source object;
- interpretation object;
- authority;
- scope;
- dependencies;
- provenance;
- uncertainty;
- version state;
- admission rules;

the interpretation evaluator must produce an equivalent constitutional result.

---

## 30. Canonical Result

The canonical result of Volume IV is:

> A traceable and authority-bounded interpretation architecture that permits explanation, comparison, translation, derivation, criticism, and implementation without allowing derived meaning to silently replace canonical meaning.
